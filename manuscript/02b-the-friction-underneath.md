# The Friction Underneath

Kathrin finished her university degree in Germany and moved to France for a postgraduate program. France wouldn't recognize the German degree. After going back and forth with a French university, she got it accepted as equivalent to a *licence* — a diploma French students earn after three years, covering, in her own account, considerably less ground than what she'd already studied. That still wasn't enough to start the postgraduate course she'd moved for. She had to complete two more years of undergraduate study in the French system first, on top of the degree she already held, before the program she'd actually come for would even consider her.

Nothing about Kathrin's move was illegal. Nothing about it required a visa, a permit, or anyone's permission — she is an EU citizen who moved to another EU country, exactly the freedom the currency union's founding treaties were built to protect. The legal right to relocate did everything it was supposed to do. It just didn't get her into a classroom.

That gap is the actual subject here. A shared currency solves arbitrage: it stops a euro from being worth something different depending on which side of a border it's spent. It was never going to solve this, because this was never a currency problem. Even EU citizens with full legal freedom of movement relocate across an internal border roughly eight times less often than Americans cross a state line, and the reasons sit in specific, nameable friction, not in any remaining legal barrier. Four of them recur across the research on this most often: whether your credentials transfer, whether your pension does, what a new home costs to find, and whether you can function in a language you may not speak.

Credential recognition is the one with the most documentation, and the most mixed picture. The EU already has a legal category for this — seven professions (doctors, nurses, midwives, dentists, pharmacists, architects, veterinary surgeons) get what the law calls "automatic recognition," with a hard statutory clock: one month for a host country to acknowledge an application, three months maximum to decide once the paperwork is complete. On paper, that's the credential-recognition equivalent of Kathrin's move — a legal right that should just work. The OECD's own research on this names the actual failure mode directly: "slow and overly heterogeneous procedures can still constitute a barrier to labour mobility, even if decisions are eventually positive." The clock can run out in your favor and the friction still costs you the years it took to get there — which is exactly what happened to Kathrin, one category down from the seven professions with a legal deadline at all.

There is a real, working answer to this, just not yet at the scale this book is asking about. Since May 2024, six countries — Belgium, the Netherlands, and Luxembourg, joined with Estonia, Latvia, and Lithuania — have been bound by a treaty that does the thing everyone keeps proposing for the whole EU: it recognizes each other's university qualification levels automatically, no case-by-case review, built on bilateral agreements the Benelux countries and the Baltic states had already run between themselves for years. Poland has applied to join. Ireland has applied to join. It is, in the words of the European University Association's own 2026 assessment of it, real progress — and also, in the same assessment's own words, a risk of becoming "the privilege of a selected few countries... a club within the club," if the circle of signatories never grows past six. The EU set a political target in 2018 for automatic recognition across the entire European Higher Education Area by 2025. That target came and went. What actually exists instead is one working regional treaty, proof the mechanism functions, not proof it has scaled.

Pension portability, housing costs, and language function differently — real, well-documented as barriers in the same OECD and European Parliament research, but without a headline number precise enough to responsibly quote here the way Kathrin's case or the treaty's six countries can be quoted. A pension earned under one country's system doesn't automatically travel with a worker the way a currency does; a worker can leave years of contributions behind, entangled in a system that assumes they'll stay. Housing costs shape moving decisions the same way they shape everything else about where people can afford to live, and a mover crossing into an unfamiliar rental market absorbs that cost with none of the local knowledge a native mover has. Language sits underneath both, unglamorous and slow to fix by policy alone — nobody has proposed a treaty for it, because there isn't one to propose.

There is no single country here that has solved this problem end to end and can be studied from start to finish the way a reader might want. What exists instead is more precise than that, and more honest: a real solution, running today, for the friction named first — credential recognition — that just isn't global yet, or even EU-wide. Whether it stays a club of six, or becomes the model the rest of Europe copies, is the open question. Not whether the mechanism works.
