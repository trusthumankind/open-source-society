# Research: Prior Art Against the Five Victory Conditions

Synthesized 2026-07-06. For each victory condition: the best existing serious research, the most instructive real-world precedent, and what a unifying blueprint would still need to originate.

## 1. One country — open borders, free movement

**Best existing research:** A mature academic literature, and the efficiency case is strikingly uncontroversial among economists who study it, even though the policy conclusion is politically radioactive. John Kennan's NBER model ([w18307](https://www.nber.org/papers/w18307)) finds open borders would produce welfare gains "about the same as the gains from a growth miracle that more than doubles the income level" in developing countries — driven by the "place premium" (the same worker earns dramatically more just by being in a higher-productivity location). Michael Clemens' widely-cited review aggregates independent model estimates at 50-150% gains to gross world product, with Klein and Ventura's model at the high end (121.69%); even Clemens' own conservative estimate is 20-60%.
**Most instructive real-world case:** No full open-borders case study exists at nation-state scale. Closest analogs are regional free-movement zones (EU Schengen, the historical US interstate labor market). The literature is overwhelmingly counterfactual/modeled, not observed at the scale this condition needs.
**What a blueprint still needs to originate:** The economics establishes the size of the prize; it says almost nothing about the transition mechanism — sequencing, the political economy of displaced domestic low-wage workers, or institutional design for managing free movement at global scale without a global sovereign. This is the single biggest originality gap across all five conditions. Worth an honest treatment of the "epidemiological case" objection (that migration could transmit poor institutions) — the actual paper making this case concludes it "could form a logically coherent efficiency case... but on current evidence... dynamically efficient policy would not imply open borders but would imply relaxation of current restrictions."

**Security/terrorism objection — engaged in `manuscript/01-open-borders.md` as of 2026-09-14.** US safety data is real and strong (Nowrasteh/Cato: 1 in 4.6M annual murder risk from foreign-born terrorism overall, 1 in 3.5B for refugees, 1 in 13.9B for Visa Waiver Program entrants, zero for illegal immigrants across 1975-2024; independently confirmed by a PNAS Texas arrest-record study and Stanford incarceration data running 1870-2020 showing immigrants, including undocumented, arrested/incarcerated at lower rates than native-born citizens). Europe's 2015-16 crisis is the real complication: DIIS Report 2017:05 counted four terrorist attacks in Europe, Jan 2016-Apr 2017, tied to that migration wave — no recognized refugees, one asylum-seeker, three people with rejected asylum claims (irregular migrants at time of attack) — against roughly 1.3 million EU asylum applications in 2015 alone. **The remaining, unresolved piece:** the safety numbers come from the most heavily-vetted entry category (US refugee resettlement: 18-24 months typical, up to 36, DHS/State/FBI/NCTC/DoD jointly screening — IRC and CFR both describe it as the most rigorous screening of any traveler), so it doesn't transfer cleanly to an unscreened "open borders" claim without a security-specific keyhole solution. Checked openborders.info's own keyhole-solutions page directly (2026-09-14): it lists tariffs, guest-worker structures, DRITI, and language/cultural-fluency requirements as active keyhole solutions — none for security or terrorism. That gap is real and still open in the literature itself, not just in this book's prior treatment of it — named as such in the manuscript rather than papered over. Sources: [Nowrasteh/Cato](https://www.cato.org/policy-analysis/terrorism-immigration-50-years-foreign-born-terrorism-us-soil-1975-2024); [PNAS Texas study](https://www.pnas.org/doi/10.1073/pnas.2014704117); [Stanford incarceration data (Rana)](https://ranabr.people.stanford.edu/sites/g/files/sbiybj26066/files/media/file/immigration_incarceration_jan2024.pdf); [DIIS Report 2017:05](https://ethz.ch/content/dam/ethz/special-interest/gess/cis/center-for-securities-studies/resources/docs/DIIS-Report_05_Europes_Refugee_Crisis_Web-1.pdf); [IRC, refugee vetting](https://www.rescue.org/how-refugee-vetting-resettlement-works); [CFR, US refugee system backgrounder](https://www.cfr.org/backgrounders/how-does-us-refugee-system-work-trump-biden-afghanistan); [openborders.info keyhole solutions](https://openborders.info/keyhole-solutions/).

## 2. One currency — no financial arbitrage between nations

**Best existing research:** Two traditions, neither resolving cleanly into "just do this." (1) Keynes' Bancor proposal for an International Clearing Union and its descendant, the IMF's Special Drawing Right (SDR, created 1967) — a basket-of-currencies unit of account, subject of renewed reform interest since 2008. (2) Optimum Currency Area theory (Mundell, McKinnon, Kenen) — the framework for when a currency union is a good idea: trade integration, labor mobility, business-cycle synchronization, fiscal coordination.
**Most instructive real-world case:** The Eurozone — a cautionary tale, not a triumphant one. Labor mobility across EU borders is roughly 8x lower than across US state lines (0.3% vs. 2.4% of population relocating annually), so asymmetric shocks (the 2010s Eurozone crisis) couldn't be absorbed the way the US absorbs regional downturns.
**What a blueprint still needs to originate:** The least "assemble, don't invent" runway of the five. SDR proposals are reserve-asset reforms for central banks, not a retail single currency for a unified world economy. Key synthesis finding, not present in any single source: the five conditions likely have a real dependency order — open borders may need to substantially precede a shared currency, not run in parallel.
**Moved out of manuscript/02-one-currency.md on 2026-08-11 (per Marty's review), now covered in manuscript/02b-the-friction-underneath.md (2026-08-27):** the specific frictions behind the 8x EU/US labor mobility gap — language, credential recognition (a German engineer's degree not automatically recognized in Athens; an Athenian nurse needing functional German before a Munich hospital would hire her), pension portability, and housing costs. None of these exist across a US state line the way they do across an EU border; a shared currency and legal right to relocate don't remove any of them. Sources: EU labor mobility barrier research (CEPS, European Parliament, OECD ECOSCOPE, Social Europe). Marty's framing: the currency chapter's job is establishing the goal and its prior art, not the friction that remains after — naming the friction there "shoots ourselves in the foot before the reader dives in." The dedicated section that resulted found real, verifiable evidence for credential recognition specifically (a documented EU case study, the Benelux-Baltic recognition treaty) but, per the same citation discipline used everywhere else in this book, described pension portability, housing costs, and language qualitatively rather than with specific percentage figures that couldn't be traced to a primary source.

## 3. Food security for every human being

**Best existing research:** SDG 2.1 ("end hunger... all year round," 2030 deadline) is the clearest existing global-consensus target. Tracking data is sober: ~733 million people hungry in 2023 (1 in 11 globally, 1 in 5 in Africa), 2.33 billion facing moderate-to-severe food insecurity — a 15-year regression to 2008-2009 levels, flat for three straight years. ~582 million projected still undernourished in 2030 if trends hold.
**Most instructive real-world case:** No single national case dominates. The UN's own framing treats this as a distribution and affordability problem (71.5% of low-income-country populations can't afford a nutritious diet even where food exists), not a scarcity problem.
**What a blueprint still needs to originate:** The target and diagnosis are already assembled by the UN system. Missing: connecting food security's stalled progress to the open-borders literature — labor mobility itself is a hunger-reduction lever nobody in the SDG 2.1 literature models.

## 4. Housing security for every human being

**Best existing research:** SDG 11.1 sets a global target, but unlike food, housing has a proven national-scale intervention model, not just a target.
**Most instructive real-world case:** Finland's Housing First policy (national, since 2008, pioneered by the Y-Foundation) — house people unconditionally first, rather than requiring sobriety/treatment/employment milestones before housing. Hostel/boarding-house populations down 76% between 2008-2017; Finland is one of the only European countries where homelessness has actually declined. The implementation model matters as much as the policy idea: ARA (national housing finance body) subsidizing homes, NGOs and municipalities coordinating directly, 15+ years of sustained non-partisan commitment.
**What a blueprint still needs to originate:** Finland is small, wealthy, high-trust, already low-inequality. The real synthesis work is the honest case for what Housing First requires to scale to poor, high-inequality, low-state-capacity contexts where most of the global unhoused population actually lives.

## 5. Education without gatekeepers

**Best existing research:** The thinnest of the five — only SDG 4.1 surfaced directly ("all girls and boys complete free, equitable and quality primary and secondary education," 2030 deadline). A real coverage gap in the research itself, not just a hard problem.
**Most instructive real-world case (used in manuscript):** Tony Byrd / IBM's degree-optional apprenticeship program, alongside the corporate skills-based-hiring literature — specifically a 2024 Harvard Business School / Burning Glass Institute study (Joseph Fuller et al.) finding fewer than 1 in 700 hires actually benefited from companies' announced degree-requirement removals, versus 18% more non-degreed hires at firms that treated it as a real hiring-process overhaul rather than a policy announcement.
**Candidates investigated and found insufficient, not incorporated:**
- **École 42** (Paris, 2013; 50+ campuses/30+ countries by 2025) — tuition-free, no-diploma-required coding school, reports "100% employment" post-core-curriculum. Complication (found 2026-08-08): ~80,000 annual applicants, only ~1.25% accepted off the original pool (~25% pass the online test, ~1,000 of ~3,000 Piscine invitees accepted), 50%+ program dropout. The 100% figure describes an already heavily-selected survivor population, not gatekeeper-free access broadly — relocates gatekeeping from credentials to endurance rather than removing it.
- **US state/federal skills-based government hiring** (found 2026-08-14) — 16+ US states have dropped degree requirements for public-sector jobs since Maryland's 2022 order; Pennsylvania's Jan 2023 executive order opened 92% of ~65,000 state jobs; federal OPM has reviewed 600+ job classifications. Government-mandated, multi-jurisdiction — a genuinely different kind of evidence than a single institution or a voluntary corporate policy. Complication: a widely-repeated figure ("almost 60% of Pennsylvania's new hires lack a degree") has no verifiable source — Pennsylvania and Maryland officials state on the record that they track *applicant* education levels, not *new-hire* education levels. Colorado is the one state that actually measures outcomes: ~80% of job classifications now emphasize skills over degrees, but only 25% of 2024 hires within those categories (1,588 people) lacked a degree, essentially flat versus 2023 — and that result took real investment (hiring-manager retraining, a workforce board), not just the policy change. Same underlying mechanism as the corporate Fuller finding, at government scale: removing the formal requirement doesn't remove the informal preference.
**What a blueprint still needs to originate:** Almost everything. "Without gatekeepers" is sharper than the SDG language — SDG 4.1 is about curriculum completion, not about removing credentialing/access gatekeepers (accreditation, admissions, cost barriers to elite institutions), which is closer to what this condition actually means. Two independent investigations (École 42, US state hiring reform) both surfaced a favorable headline that didn't survive checking the actual outcome data, not just the policy/program description — this condition's real gap may be structural (nobody has found a genuine large-scale case where removing a credential gate produced broad, unselected access) rather than just under-researched. Needs its own dedicated research pass before the blueprint can responsibly claim coverage.

## Cross-cutting: the poverty roadmap

UN Special Rapporteur on extreme poverty Olivier De Schutter spent 18 months building "A Roadmap for Eradicating Poverty Beyond Growth," presented to the UN Human Rights Council's 62nd session (2026) — explicitly framed as expanding the policy toolkit beyond growth-based poverty reduction. This is the single most structurally relevant precedent found: an actual attempt, backed by UN mandate, at roadmap-level synthesis across poverty-adjacent domains — closer to what this book is attempting than any single-domain source. The 2025 SDG progress report's own verdict on the 2030 Agenda: "improved millions of lives" but off-track, with conflict, climate, inequality, and debt servicing named as compounding headwinds across every domain, not just one.
